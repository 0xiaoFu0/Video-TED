Overview

Recognizing unseen actions through hierarchical visual evidence

Recognizing an unseen activity depends on identifying its sub-actions and the visible evidence that connects them. ActionTree-97k organizes that evidence into verified action trees. VideoTED uses those trees during post-training to guide token-level updates and teach reference responses.

Related work

Prior work on fine-grained actions describes sub-motions, body states or compositional labels. VideoTED's tree makes the evidence–sub-action–activity links explicit for individual clips. Its training design combines token-level credit modulation on student samples with reference supervision when sampled answers fail.

ActionTree-97k

From observations to activities

Each Hierarchical Action Tree has visible observations at its leaves, sub-actions at its branches and an activity at its root. Uncertain details are recorded separately from confirmed evidence.

Generation and verification

  1. Generate four candidate trees from sampled frames without revealing the target label.
  2. Check schema, visual observations and parent–child support. Verify the activity in a separate pass.
  3. Build teacher context and a reference response from a retained tree, and filter overlaps with evaluation data.

Human inspection found 936 of 1,000 sampled annotations acceptable. The paper reports zero shared videos across all eight evaluation benchmarks and zero shared novel classes across the four OVAR benchmarks.

Method

Two ways to learn from verified evidence

Each tree yields privileged context for a self-teacher and a separate reference response for the student. During inference, the student receives the video and question.

TCM

Tree-Conditioned Credit Modulation

Teacher context

The self-teacher reads the verified action tree and activity label alongside the video and question. It compares its scores with the student’s scores for tokens in the student’s sampled responses.

Token-level updates

Scores at the observation, sub-action and activity levels adjust how strongly each token is updated. The group reward determines the update direction.

RTL

Reference Trajectory Learning

Reference construction

Each verified tree supplies a reference analysis and final answer. The student learns to produce that response from the video and question.

Failure-aware weighting

The reference loss increases with the squared group failure rate. Reference responses are trained separately from the sampled responses used for reward and advantage calculations.

Main VideoTED-7B training configuration

Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, one epoch on 32 A800 GPUs (80 GB), AdamW with a cosine schedule and learning rate 1 × 10−6, BF16 with FSDP. Each update uses 32 prompts and four rollouts per prompt. Training video input uses 2 FPS, at most 16 frames, and a 100,352-pixel budget.

The initial TCM coefficient is 0.5 and decays over 1,000 steps. The teacher uses 0.99 EMA retention. The RTL coefficient is 0.1 and continues after TCM ends. Student prompt, teacher prompt, sampled response and reference limits are 16,384, 18,432, 768 and 1,024 tokens, respectively.

Experiments

Results across eight benchmarks

VideoTED-7B improves on Qwen2.5-VL-7B across the four open-vocabulary datasets and all eight overall and action metrics reported for the general video benchmarks.

Evaluation protocol

In open-vocabulary action recognition, the model chooses a label from the base or novel partition of K400, HMDB51, UCF101 or SSv2. Evaluation reports each partition’s accuracy and their harmonic mean. In MVBench, Video-MME, TempCompass and the SEED-Bench video subset, the paper reports both the full benchmark score and a designated action-related score. Video-MME uses no subtitles.

Model and data scaling

Post-training improves the reported metrics across Qwen2.5-VL-3B/7B and Qwen3-VL-4B/8B. The data sweep compares 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% of ActionTree-97k. The full-data 7B run clears the base model across the three plotted metrics.

Training dynamics and sensitivity

When all sampled rewards match, the group-relative policy advantage vanishes. RTL supplies an independent reference objective when the group fails. The supplement compares coefficient choices, hierarchy shuffling and human inspection criteria.

Qualitative results

Inspect the evidence

Compare selected frames, ground truth and saved model outputs for temporal ordering, action direction and changes in object state. These examples show saved predictions and their supporting frames.

Failure case: fast motion vs. fast playback

On a TempCompass basketball clip played at normal speed, VideoTED interpreted motion blur and fast player movements as fast-forward playback. The supplement also notes that a correct final answer can include details that the sampled frames cannot fully establish.

Limitations and future work

Automatically verified trees can still carry annotation errors into both training paths. Fixed-rate frame sampling can miss brief contacts and intermediate states. The supplement discusses adaptive keyframe selection and explicit temporal or spatial grounding.
